# Chapter 1 — Low Light Gospel

---

The sun was the only light for three million kilometers.

Something was burning toward it. Not falling — the physics were wrong for falling. Every correction point in the trajectory said the same thing: the ship had been aimed there. Whoever was flying had made that choice, and then made it again and again, at every altitude where they could have turned back, until there were no more altitudes left.

The crowd spread across six levels of the lower decks and across transit screens and across the upper city balconies where people had stopped mid-stride when Zel's signal broke into every channel at once. It wasn't only the lower decks this time. The image was everywhere — the kind of everywhere that only happened when something had occurred that the official feeds couldn't get ahead of.

A ship burning.

A trajectory no living pilot had run before, burning.

And the debris, catching the corona light, becoming something brighter than wreckage had any right to be.

Zel's signal ran no commentary. No voice. Just the image and the data scrolling at the edge of the frame: velocity, altitude, the last known position of the wings before the wings were no longer a single thing at a single position, before they were everywhere and nowhere, spread across a debris field that was going to be visible from the lower decks for days.

No name.

Zel's signal gave no name.

Maybe the name was already moving through the lower decks the way certain names moved — not from any one source, just spreading, just present, the way heat spread before you found the fire. Or maybe Zel understood that saying the name would make it a fact when right now it was something larger than a fact. Six million people watching from six million different locations, and every one of them deciding what they were looking at, and the name already in all of them whether anyone said it or not.

The wreckage burned.

The crowd watched.

Somewhere in the lower decks, someone said the name. Quietly. The way you'd say the last line of something you'd been reading a long time.

The people near them heard.

*Six years earlier.*

---

The cargo was three sealed units, no manifest, eight hundred in unlicensed composite components — the kind of run that didn't ask you to feel anything about it. Pay in, cargo handled, payout at First Pull. The lower decks ran on this kind of transaction. The lower decks ran on a lot of things that didn't officially exist, which was the primary reason Mercy had steady work.

He ran the pre-flight in the dark of the cockpit and tried not to feel anything about it. This was his primary professional failing, or one of three running for the position.

*Pre-flight reads clean,* Juno said through the comms. *Port thruster is point-three below nominal — not a problem, just flagging.*

"Flagged."

*Cargo's sealed and locked. Route confirmation's in the system.*

"I confirmed it."

*You confirmed something. We'll find out which thing when you come back.*

He didn't respond to this, because responding would require confirming a route he was already planning to modify, and Juno had a specific patience for being managed that he had no interest in testing at pre-flight.

He lifted out of the bay and went south.

From the air the lower decks had a geography that the ground didn't give you. From the ground they were noise and weight and the amber of the secondary lighting, everything too close and too loud and too present. From the air they opened up into a map: the residential sectors to the west, packed warm, windows too close together; the commercial corridors running north-south, cargo docks at three-hundred-meter intervals; the maintenance bays where licensed mechanics did licensed work, and further south the bays where work happened that didn't file paperwork. He'd spent twenty years in this geography before he'd ever been in the air. He'd known every corridor by foot. From the air it was like reading a map of a city you'd only ever walked through — the same thing, entirely different.

Above him, past his current altitude, past the secondary transit lanes, was the sky that cost money to be in.

He could see it from here. The syndicate's registered lanes: lit in the blue-white of the primary grid, ships moving in the clean patterns that had been negotiated and taxed and permitted. Every ship in those lanes had a registry number, a flight plan, an authorization code purchased from the transit authority at rates that had increased fourteen percent in the last two years. The transit authority was a function of the syndicate. The syndicate owned the lanes the way it owned most things: completely, and with documentation.

He flew below all of it. Everyone in the lower decks flew below all of it.

Corridor seven came into range ahead — standard approach, clean geometry, the approved route running him in at a regulated altitude that wouldn't trouble the proximity sensors or the warden drones, that would deliver the cargo without incident. Nothing wrong with it. A perfectly reasonable corridor.

He looked at the entrance to corridor nine, two hundred meters north.

Corridor nine was restricted to registered upper-transit ships. Ships with authorization codes. Ships with the fees paid. In the relevant sense, corridor nine was not open to him.

He flew into it anyway.

*Mercy.*

"More efficient approach."

*Corridor seven is the efficient approach.*

"There's a geometry issue on the seven entry I want to avoid."

*I'm looking at the corridor seven geometry. There is no issue.*

"I'm looking at it from the cockpit."

*From the cockpit of a ship that is in corridor nine.*

He didn't answer. There wasn't an answer that helped him.

Corridor nine ran north-to-south at an altitude low enough that the pedestrian platform noise ordinance applied. Low enough that the platforms had full line of sight. The shift change ran mid-afternoon, which was now — workers moving between sectors, people with somewhere to be in thirty minutes who currently had nowhere particular to be. He'd timed it. He'd timed it a month ago when he'd first scouted the route, and he'd timed it again this morning when he filed the route confirmation, and the fact that he'd done both of these things was a piece of self-knowledge he had decided not to examine directly.

He came down to the ordinance altitude. Then below it — not recklessly, with precision, because the margin he was flying was a margin he'd calculated, and he flew calculated margins the way other people breathed.

Then he went fast.

The corridor was rated for a speed he was not doing. The pressure differential off the hull at this altitude, moving at this speed, was something you could feel on the platform below — the air having to move somewhere in a hurry, which meant a sound and a displacement that the noise ordinance existed specifically to prevent. He knew this. He was exploiting it deliberately, which was a version of himself he was entirely at peace with and mildly concerned about in equal measure.

The crowd below went still. He didn't look down. He never looked. Looking down was what people did when they needed to confirm someone was watching, and confirmation was not the same thing as knowing, and knowing was not the same thing as using. He could feel the stillness through the ship the way you felt a change in pressure before your ears caught up — the weight of fifty people stopping mid-step, looking up at something that shouldn't be there, something moving too fast for this corridor, something they hadn't been told to expect.

He used it like a landmark. Aligned himself against it. Let it be the north end of the corridor and came through tight on the south exit, two meters of clearance on each side, everything exactly where he'd put it.

He came out clean.

*Dock is ready,* Juno said. Flat now. The particular flat that meant she had something to say and had chosen a location for it marked *not yet.*

"Copy."

The dock ahead, geometry straightforward. He read a wind off the secondary exhaust arrays — not much, just enough to factor — and adjusted, came in at the correct angle, correct speed, set the cargo down inside the tolerance band. Everything by the numbers. Everything clean.

The ship settled. The engines began their cooling sequence. The dock came back to him in pieces — the loaders moving in, the cargo scanner running, the ambient noise of people who had work and were doing it.

He sat in the cockpit and looked at the platform indicator on his display. The pedestrian platforms south of corridor nine.

Already gone.

He'd been on the ground for forty seconds.

He climbed down.

---

Juno was at the foot of the ramp.

She was always at the foot of the ramp — not coincidence, because Juno didn't operate on coincidence. She operated on logistics: the calculation of where she needed to be and when, the arrangement of herself in space so that things ran correctly. Two years running his ground operations had given her a precise read on his approach patterns, which meant she'd had the dock crew staged to his descent vector before he'd filed his final clearance, which meant she'd already run the math on what he'd actually do and prepared for it without telling him she'd done any of this.

She handed him a cup. Something warm and adjacent to the thing it was claiming to be, in the lower-decks tradition of beverages.

He drank it.

"The obstruction in corridor seven," she said.

"Cleared by the time I got there."

"Funny timing."

"Happens."

She looked at him the way she looked at things she'd decided not to argue yet — not letting it go, not surfacing it now, just logging it in the place where she kept things she'd be returning to. Juno had significant storage in that place. He was the primary contributor.

The cargo was already moving. The dock crew ran the seals without needing direction, because that was how Juno ran a dock: you arrived and everything was already in motion. The food vendor two spots past her permit limit had the usual smell going — oil and spice and something underneath that was probably optimism. Someone three docks over was arguing with a scanner. The secondary lighting did its amber thing to the air.

"You got the crowd," Juno said.

Not a question. She'd run the corridor nine geometry and she knew exactly what his altitude over the platform had been and what that meant at shift change.

"Yeah," he said.

"And?"

He looked at his cup. Turned it. "Forty seconds. Maybe fifty. The platform cleared before I'd landed."

"I know."

Not *I know, that's unfortunate.* Not *I know, that's what always happens.* Just the two words, in the tone that meant she'd known this was coming before he'd left, and she'd let him go anyway, and she was watching him arrive at the same place she'd been waiting at this whole time.

The thing was: it should have been enough. Two years in the lower corridors, the best unauthorized times anyone was running, the margins getting tighter and the routes getting faster and his read on the lower-decks geometry getting sharper until he could walk a corridor once and know what it would feel like at speed. He was twenty years old and he was the best pilot in any corridor he flew and the lower decks' ceiling was right above him.

The ceiling was not enough.

He was aware of what this said about him. He had an entire file on what it said about him, had been adding to it for two years, and the file was not flattering, and he kept doing the same things anyway. Forty seconds. The crowd was already gone before he'd finished landing — not every time, but enough times that he'd stopped being surprised by it, and stopping being surprised was its own kind of problem.

"You're not going to say anything about corridor nine," he said.

"Not right now."

"When?"

She looked at him steadily. "When you're ready to hear it."

This was the thing about Juno: she had no interest in saying something to someone who wasn't going to take it in. She'd learned the difference between the person in front of her and the person who could actually receive what she was saying, and she had the patience to wait for the gap to close. He'd spent a long time thinking this was indifference. He'd worked out eventually that it was the opposite.

He finished the cup and handed it back to her and she took it, because someone always had to, and it was always her.

---

He was standing at the port access wall, positioned near the corridor entrance in the way of someone who'd placed himself where he had a clear sightline without being in the operational path. An older man — sixty, maybe a few years past — with the stillness that belonged to people who had been very busy earlier and had learned, at real cost, how to stop. He wore a mechanic's coverall that had been washed enough times to go soft. He had a tablet he wasn't looking at.

He was looking at the dock approach. Specifically at the angle where corridor nine's south exit aligned with the eastern dock entry — the angle you'd need to be standing at to have watched Mercy come through.

Mercy walked over.

"The altitude adjustment at the north end of the corridor," the man said, before Mercy could say anything. "That was for the platform crowd. Not for the exit geometry."

Mercy studied him. "I don't know you."

"Kweku." Just the name. No extension offered. "I've been watching your runs."

"People don't generally tell me that."

"I'm not generally telling anyone." He looked at the tablet briefly — not handing it over, just referencing it. "Your instinct corrections run faster than your training accounts for. Your pattern reads are better than they should be at your hours. You have a quality that doesn't come from practice." He paused. "And your ceiling is wrong."

Mercy waited for the rest of it. The *too low, too fast, the wardens are going to catch you* part. The version of this conversation he'd had eight times in two years.

"Too low," Kweku said. "The ceiling you're flying at. It's set wrong."

That was not the version of this conversation he'd had before.

"I have something I'd like to show you," Kweku said. "It's not far."

"What is it?"

He considered this — the specific consideration of someone deciding which true version of an answer to give. "It changes what flying means," he said. "I can't be more precise than that until you've seen it."

Mercy glanced at Juno. She was doing the thing where she was listening without appearing to be listening, the operational skill she'd developed to a high level. She gave him nothing — not encouragement, not warning. Just her face in neutral, which meant she'd run her accounting of the situation and landed on *your call.*

Juno's neutral was not the same as regular neutral. He'd spent two years learning the difference.

He looked back at Kweku. The dock went about its business. The cargo was sealed and moving. The vendor's smell came from three spots past where it had a right to be. His engines were done cooling.

The run was one of the cleanest he'd run. Forty seconds on the platform and already gone. He'd been calling what came after *not yet* for two years, and *not yet* was doing a significant amount of work that he hadn't hired it to do. Eventually was running out of time to mean later. He was twenty years old in the lower decks. Eventually had a horizon.

"Alright," he said.

Kweku nodded once — the nod of someone who had expected exactly this answer and had waited for it without urgency — and moved toward the maintenance access.

Mercy followed.

The workshop was two levels below the dock: a maintenance bay that had been cleared of its registered equipment and filled with something else. Kweku didn't hurry. He walked the way people walked when they'd decided where they were going a long time ago and didn't need the movement to confirm it. The corridors were the regular lower-decks kind — amber-lit, close-walled, smelling of the city's recycled breath.

Then the smell changed.

Not suddenly. Gradually, over the last fifty meters before the door. Something underneath the standard mechanic's heat and oil — something that had been worked on obsessively, over time, by someone who had stopped tracking what hour it was. Metal and heat and something else he didn't have a name for, something his body registered the way it registered a change in air pressure before his ears caught up.

Kweku opened the door.

Mercy walked in.

The door closed behind them.
